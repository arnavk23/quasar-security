\title{When Do Amplitude-Encoding Quantum Kernels Help Security Anomaly Detection? A Matched-Scale Study on QKD Telemetry and Network Intrusion Data}

\author{Arnav~Kapoor%
\thanks{A. Kapoor is with the Department of Computer Science, Indian Institute of Science Education and Research Bhopal, Bhopal 462066, India (e-mail arnavkapoor23@iiserb.ac.in).}}

\markboth{Manuscript}{Kapoor, Amplitude-Encoding Kernels for Security Anomaly Detection}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Amplitude encoding is the most common way to load data into quantum kernels
proposed for security tasks. Its fidelity kernel equals the squared cosine
similarity of the inputs. It can therefore be computed classically at any scale
and compared fairly with classical kernels. We do this on four datasets in two
domains. The first is QKD telemetry from a decoy-state BB84 simulator with
operating drift. The second is network flows from a synthetic zero-trust
simulator, UNSW-NB15 and CIC-IDS2017. Every model is trained on the same data at
every training size. In distribution, the amplitude-encoding SVM never beats an
RBF-kernel SVM and needs more data at small $n$. Tree ensembles and neural
networks lead. On attack types held out from training the result depends on the
data. In both controlled settings the kernel fails, because unseen attacks differ
from normal traffic mainly in magnitude and the kernel ignores magnitude. A
one-coordinate norm anchor repairs this. On the two public benchmarks it does not
fail. A simple norm statistic does not predict which case applies. Held-out
attack tests on real traffic are therefore needed. We also find that a
Gaussian-process posterior gives worse alert deferral than an isotonic-calibrated
random forest, and that drift-free QKD simulators are trivially separable.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Quantum machine learning, quantum kernels, amplitude encoding, intrusion detection, quantum key distribution, zero-day attacks, calibration

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{Q}{uantum} kernel methods map inputs to quantum states and use state
overlaps as a kernel~\cite{havlicek2019supervised,schuld2021machine}. They are
being proposed for security tasks such as intrusion
detection~\cite{kalinin2023security} and botnet
detection~\cite{suryotrisongko2022evaluating}.

Two facts make such results hard to read. Whether a quantum kernel helps depends
on how its geometry matches the data~\cite{huang2021power}. Proven advantages
exist only for specially built problems~\cite{liu2021rigorous}. Quantum models are
also often trained on fewer samples than the baselines, because circuit
simulation is slow.

We study amplitude encoding, the most common encoding in this literature. Its
kernel is classically computable (Proposition~\ref{prop:exact}). So we can train it
at any size, on the same data as classical kernels. Our question does not depend
on quantum hardware. Is its inductive bias useful for security anomaly detection,
and when?

We answer it in two domains. The first is QKD telemetry, where implementation
attacks~\cite{huttner1995photon,lydersen2010hacking,gisin2006trojan,bennett1992experimental}
perturb channel statistics. The second is flow monitoring for zero-trust
networks~\cite{rose2020zero,syed2022zero}, on a simulator and on UNSW-NB15 and
CIC-IDS2017~\cite{moustafa2015unsw,sharafaldin2018toward}.

Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A matched-scale comparison across four datasets in two domains.
\item A zero-day failure of the kernel in both controlled settings, traced to its
  norm invariance by interventions.
\item A one-coordinate norm anchor that repairs the failure without tuning on
  held-out attacks.
\item Evidence that the failure does not occur on two public benchmarks, and that
  a simple statistic cannot predict it.
\item Evidence that a GP posterior is a worse basis for alert deferral than an
  isotonic-calibrated random forest.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}

\textbf{Quantum kernels.} A quantum kernel evaluates
$k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=|\langle\psi(\mathbf{x})|\psi(\mathbf{y})\rangle|^2$ for a
data-dependent state~\cite{havlicek2019supervised}. Entangling feature maps can be
hard to evaluate classically~\cite{liu2021rigorous}. Even then they need not beat
classical learners on real data~\cite{huang2021power}. Amplitude encoding is the
easy case. Any benefit it gives must come from its inductive bias. Nystr\"om
methods~\cite{williams2001nystrom} and random features~\cite{rahimi2007random} let
any such kernel scale. Lu et al.\ study adversarial attacks on quantum
classifiers~\cite{lu2020quantum}.

\textbf{QKD attack detection.} Defenses include hardware monitoring of detector
statistics~\cite{dasilva2012monitoring} and protocol changes such as decoy
states~\cite{lo2005decoy} and measurement-device-independent
QKD~\cite{lo2012measurement}. Mao et al.\ detect attacks on continuous-variable
QKD with neural networks~\cite{mao2020detecting}. Ren et al.\ use machine learning
to choose QKD protocols~\cite{ren2021machine}. These studies run on simulated
telemetry.

\textbf{Intrusion detection.} Machine-learning intrusion detection is surveyed by
Buczak and Guven~\cite{buczak2016survey}. Sommer and Paxson warn that detectors
often fail on attacks outside their training set~\cite{sommer2010outside}. This is
why we test held-out attack types. Kitsune popularized autoencoders that never
see attack labels~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}.

\textbf{Calibration and robustness.} Operators act on alert confidence, so
calibration matters~\cite{guo2017calibration}. Gaussian
processes~\cite{rasmussen2006gaussian} and other Bayesian
models~\cite{perusquia2022bayesian} give model-based uncertainty. Platt
scaling~\cite{platt1999probabilistic} and isotonic
regression~\cite{zadrozny2002transforming} recalibrate other models. Randomized
smoothing~\cite{cohen2019certified} certifies $\ell_2$ robustness.

\section{The Amplitude-Encoding Kernel}
\label{sec:kernel}

Amplitude encoding pads $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ with zeros to $2^q$ entries,
with $q=\lceil\log_2 d\rceil$. It then normalizes the result into the state
$|\psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle=\sum_i \hat{x}_i|i\rangle$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:exact}
The fidelity kernel of amplitude encoding is
\begin{equation}
K_A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = |\langle\psi(\mathbf{x})|\psi(\mathbf{y})\rangle|^2
= \frac{(\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{y})^2}{\|\mathbf{x}\|^2\,\|\mathbf{y}\|^2}.
\label{eq:ka}
\end{equation}
It costs $\mathcal{O}(d)$ per pair.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The amplitudes are real and the padding is zero. So the overlap equals
$\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathbf{y}}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:inv}
$K_A(c\,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=K_A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})$ for every $c\neq0$. A
classifier built on $K_A$ gives $\mathbf{x}$, $2\mathbf{x}$ and $-\mathbf{x}$ the
same score.
\end{corollary}

On standardized features the origin is the training mean. So the kernel cannot
use how far an input lies from typical data. Corollary~\ref{cor:inv} predicts
that $K_A$ helps when labels depend on direction alone. It predicts harm when an
attack stands out mainly by being far from normal traffic.

\textbf{Norm anchor.} We append a constant before encoding,
\begin{equation}
\phi_c(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{(\mathbf{x},\,c)}{\sqrt{\|\mathbf{x}\|^2+c^2}},
\qquad c=\operatorname{median}_{\mathrm{train}}\|\mathbf{x}\| .
\label{eq:anchor}
\end{equation}
The last amplitude falls as $\|\mathbf{x}\|$ grows. The sign ambiguity disappears.
The median rule sets $c$ from training data alone. It costs one extra amplitude.

\textbf{Sensitivity.} The Jacobian of $\mathbf{x}\mapsto\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ has spectral
norm exactly $1/\|\mathbf{x}\|$. Normalization shrinks perturbations of large inputs
and magnifies those near the origin. It gives no uniform robustness guarantee.

\textbf{Implementation check.} We compare Eq.~\eqref{eq:ka} with a
PennyLane~\cite{bergholm2018pennylane} simulation of the overlap circuit. They
agree to $7\times10^{-15}$. The circuit takes 26\,ms per kernel entry and the
closed form $3\times10^{-4}$\,ms, about $8\times10^4$ times faster. Training sizes
of a few dozen samples reported for such kernels reflect circuit simulation, not
the kernel.

\textbf{QKD telemetry.} Each sample is one key-generation block of a decoy-state
BB84 link over 50\,km of fiber~\cite{lo2005decoy}. Normal operation drifts in
loss, efficiency, misalignment, dark counts and background light. The error-rate
estimates carry finite-size noise. Four attacks perturb seven measurements. Each
perturbation is scaled by a strength $s$. We report $s=1$ and the harder $s=0.3$.
Features are the seven measurements plus a robust Mahalanobis
distance~\cite{rousseeuw1999fast} to normal blocks. Without drift, a single
feature separated attacks perfectly in our first version. We release leakage
tests with the simulator.

\textbf{Synthetic flows.} Flow vectors follow NetFlow-like marginals coupled by a
Gaussian copula. Five attacks each shift 6 to 7 latent dimensions.

\textbf{Public data.} UNSW-NB15 uses its official split. CIC-IDS2017 is cleaned of
missing values, infinite values and 308,381 duplicates. Classes under 100 flows
are dropped and the rest form six families. Features pass through
$\operatorname{sign}(x)\log(1+|x|)$.

\textbf{Models.} AE-SVM uses $K_A$. AE-SVM (anchored) uses $K_A$ on $\phi_c$. AE-SVM
(nonneg) scales inputs to $[0,1]$ first. Classical kernels are RBF, Laplacian and
cubic polynomial, all in the same SVM. Above 1,500 samples we use a Nystr\"om
approximation. Other models are random forests (RF)~\cite{breiman2001random},
XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} and an MLP. On public data we add a deep network
(DNN) and a benign-only autoencoder~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}. On QKD data we add a
Laplace GP, a GP and AE-SVM stacking model (Hybrid)~\cite{wolpert1992stacked},
isotonic RF and three novelty detectors.

\textbf{Protocol.} Every model in a comparison is trained on the same subsample at
every size. Zero-day tests train on all but one attack type and test on the held-out
type. We report ROC-AUC there, since it needs no threshold tuned on known attacks.
Intervals are 95\% confidence intervals over seeds.

QKD data first looks like a counter-example. There AE-SVM does well. But the QKD

closed-world warning of Sommer and Paxson~\cite{sommer2010outside}.

\subsection{Mechanism}
\label{sec:res-mechanism}

Corollary~\ref{cor:inv} offers two causes, sign ambiguity and norm invariance.
Interventions separate them (Table~\ref{tab:zd}). Scaling to $[0,1]$ removes the
sign ambiguity but keeps norm invariance. It helps little on synthetic flows. The
anchor restores the norm. It closes most of the gap on synthetic flows and all of
it on QKD data without the Mahalanobis feature. On public data it changes little.

\subsection{Robustness and cost}
\label{sec:res-robust}

Randomized smoothing gives similar certified radii for AE-SVM and RBF-SVM on
synthetic flows, 0.508 and 0.518 on average. AE-SVM abstains on 1.8\% of points
and RBF-SVM on none. This fits the sensitivity result of Section~\ref{sec:kernel}.

With 50 Nystr\"om landmarks AE-SVM costs 0.0048\,ms per synthetic flow, about 1.2
times faster than RBF-SVM. With 1000 landmarks on public data every kernel SVM is
slower than XGBoost, the MLP and the autoencoder. All models stay far below a
per-flow budget. Our timings come from a shared machine and show ordering only.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Meaning.} Amplitude encoding gives no in-distribution gain over an RBF
kernel on any of four datasets. At small $n$ it needs more data. Its defining
property is an invariance. It sees direction and ignores magnitude. That helps when
magnitude is noise. It hurts when unseen attacks differ from normal traffic mainly
in size, as in both controlled settings. On real traffic, attack families also
differ in which features are active. There the weakness did not appear.

Nothing here bears on quantum speed-ups, since $K_A$ is classically easy. The
invariance argument applies to any fidelity kernel whose state preparation
normalizes its input. Simulators alone would have led us to the wrong general
conclusion. The public benchmarks reversed it.

\textbf{Recommendations.} We suggest five practices for testing quantum or
quantum-style detectors.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Compare against classical kernels trained on the same data at every $n$.
\item Report held-out attack results on real traffic.
\item Check what the kernel is invariant to, and whether a feature restores it.
\item If amplitude encoding is used, anchor the norm. In our data it fixed every
  failure and cost at most 0.005 AUC elsewhere.
\item For simulated telemetry, report single-feature separability and a strength
  sweep.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Threats to validity.} QKD attack sizes are modeling assumptions, and no
public QKD attack telemetry exists to our knowledge. The flow simulator is not
packet-level traffic. Both public datasets have known labelling artifacts. We
subsample them to 20,000 training flows, which favours kernel methods. Kernel
settings were not tuned per dataset for any kernel. Tuning might change small gaps
but is unlikely to close the 0.3 AUC zero-day gaps. Holding out one attack family
only approximates a truly new attack. Certified radii cover $\ell_2$ noise, not
adaptive evasion.

\section{Conclusion}

We tested the amplitude-encoding kernel exactly, at every training size, against
classical kernels on the same data. It never beats an RBF kernel in distribution.
It needs more data at small $n$. In both controlled settings it fails on unseen
attacks, because it discards input norm. A one-coordinate norm anchor repairs
this. On both public benchmarks it does not fail. Quantum detectors should be
judged on held-out attack families from real traffic, with their invariances
checked first.
