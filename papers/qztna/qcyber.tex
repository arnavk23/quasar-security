\begin{abstract}
Amplitude encoding is the most common way to load data into quantum kernels
proposed for intrusion detection. Its fidelity kernel equals the squared cosine
similarity of the inputs, so it can be computed classically at any scale. We
compare it with classical kernels, tree ensembles and neural networks. Every
model is trained on the same data at every training size. We use a synthetic
zero-trust flow simulator, UNSW-NB15 and CIC-IDS2017. In distribution, the
amplitude-encoding SVM (AE-SVM) ties an RBF-kernel SVM and trails tree
ensembles. On attack types held out from training, AE-SVM fails on synthetic
flows, with mean AUC $0.526$ against $0.839$ for RBF-SVM. Controls show the cause
is the kernel's invariance to input norm. Appending one constant coordinate
before encoding restores the AUC to $0.793$ and also raises in-distribution
accuracy. On the public benchmarks the failure does not appear. Mean held-out AUC
is $0.945$ against $0.932$ for RBF-SVM on UNSW-NB15 and $0.715$ against $0.658$ on CIC-IDS2017. Held-out-attack tests on real traffic are therefore needed
before judging such kernels.
\end{abstract}

Quantum kernels, amplitude encoding, intrusion detection, zero trust, zero-day attacks

\section{Introduction}

Zero-trust architectures check every flow before granting access~\cite{rose2020zero,syed2022zero}.
This makes flow-level anomaly detection part of each access decision.

Quantum kernel methods map inputs to quantum states and use state overlaps as a
kernel~\cite{havlicek2019supervised,schuld2021machine}. They have been applied to
intrusion detection~\cite{kalinin2023security} and botnet
detection~\cite{suryotrisongko2022evaluating}. Such evaluations often use a single
operating point. The quantum model is often trained on fewer samples than the
classical baselines, because circuit simulation is slow.

We test amplitude encoding under a matched protocol. Every model sees the same
training data at every size. We also hold out whole attack types, since a
deployed detector will meet attacks it was not trained on~\cite{sommer2010outside}.

Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A matched comparison on one simulator and two public benchmarks
  (Tables~\ref{tab:indist} and~\ref{tab:scale}).
\item A zero-day failure of AE-SVM on synthetic flows, traced to norm invariance
  by controlled interventions (Tables~\ref{tab:zd} and~\ref{tab:sweep}).
\item A one-coordinate norm anchor that repairs the failure without tuning on
  held-out attacks.
\item Evidence that the failure does not occur on UNSW-NB15 or CIC-IDS2017.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Amplitude-Encoding Kernel}
\label{sec:method}

Amplitude encoding pads $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ with zeros to $2^q$ entries,
with $q=\lceil\log_2 d\rceil$. It then normalizes the vector into the state
$|\psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle$. The fidelity kernel is
\begin{equation}
K_A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = |\langle\psi(\mathbf{x})|\psi(\mathbf{y})\rangle|^2
= \frac{(\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{y})^2}{\|\mathbf{x}\|^2\,\|\mathbf{y}\|^2}.
\label{eq:ka}
\end{equation}
It costs $\mathcal{O}(d)$ per pair on a classical computer. No quantum hardware
is needed.

Eq.~\eqref{eq:ka} gives $K_A(c\,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=K_A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})$ for any
$c\neq0$. So a classifier built on $K_A$ scores $\mathbf{x}$, $2\mathbf{x}$ and
$-\mathbf{x}$ the same. On standardized features the origin is the training
mean. The kernel therefore cannot use how far a flow lies from typical traffic.

\textbf{Norm anchor.} We append a constant before encoding,
\begin{equation}
\phi_c(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{(\mathbf{x},\,c)}{\sqrt{\|\mathbf{x}\|^2+c^2}},
\qquad c = \operatorname{median}_{\mathrm{train}} \|\mathbf{x}\| .
\label{eq:anchor}
\end{equation}
The last amplitude now falls as $\|\mathbf{x}\|$ grows. The sign of $\mathbf{x}$ is
no longer ambiguous. The median rule fixes $c$ from training data alone.

\textbf{Sensitivity.} The Jacobian of $\mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathbf{x}/\|\mathbf{x}\|$ has
spectral norm exactly $1/\|\mathbf{x}\|$. Normalization shrinks perturbations of
large inputs and magnifies those near the origin. It gives no uniform bound on
sensitivity.

\textbf{Classifier.} We train SVMs on $K_A$ and on classical kernels with the same
settings ($C=1$, balanced class weights). Above 1,500 training samples we use the
Nystr\"om approximation~\cite{williams2001nystrom}. Platt scaling gives
probabilities~\cite{platt1999probabilistic}. Randomized
smoothing~\cite{cohen2019certified} gives certified $\ell_2$ radii.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{Datasets. Public data uses 20,000 training and 50,000 test flows per seed.}
  \label{tab:data}
  \footnotesize

  \begin{tabular}{lrrrr}

    \midrule

    UNSW-NB15 & 257,673 & 68 & 9 & 5 \\
    CIC-IDS2017 & 2,520,730 & 69 & 6 & 5 \\
    \bottomrule

Table~\ref{tab:data} lists the data. The synthetic flows follow NetFlow-like
marginals coupled by a Gaussian copula. Each of five attacks shifts 6 to 7
latent dimensions. UNSW-NB15~\cite{moustafa2015unsw} uses its official
train and test split. CIC-IDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018toward} is cleaned of
missing values, infinite values and 308,381 duplicates. Classes with fewer than
100 flows are dropped. The rest are grouped into six families with a fixed 70/30
split. Public features pass through
$\operatorname{sign}(x)\log(1+|x|)$. All features are standardized on the training
data.

Baselines are RBF, Laplacian and cubic polynomial SVMs, random forest
(RF)~\cite{breiman2001random}, XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} and a small MLP. On
public data we add a $3\times256$ deep network (DNN) and an autoencoder trained on
benign flows only, in the style of Kitsune~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}. We report
means with 95\% confidence intervals over seeds.

Table~\ref{tab:indist} shows that AE-SVM and RBF-SVM are tied. On synthetic flows
their accuracies are $0.947$ and $0.948$ (paired $t$-test, $p=0.54$). On UNSW-NB15

($p=0.001$). Tree ensembles and neural networks lead on every dataset. The
anchored encoding is the best kernel on synthetic flows. It matches plain AE-SVM
on public data.

Table~\ref{tab:zd} holds the main result. On synthetic flows plain AE-SVM fails on
every held-out type (Fig.~\ref{fig:zd}). Its AUC lies between $0.45$ and $0.61$,

The two controls isolate the cause. Scaling inputs to $[0,1]$ removes the sign

norm. It closes most of the gap to RBF-SVM.

AE-SVM matches or beats RBF-SVM on held-out families. On CIC-IDS2017 most
supervised models struggle on Bot and PortScan. The benign-only autoencoder, which
never sees an attack, is the strongest detector there.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

AE-SVM sees direction and ignores magnitude. This helps when magnitude is noise.
It hurts when unseen attacks differ from normal traffic mainly in size, as in our
simulator. On real traffic, attack families also differ in which features are
active. There the weakness did not appear. A simulator alone would have led us to
the wrong general conclusion.

Our companion study of QKD telemetry finds the same mechanism~\cite{kapoor2026hyqad}.
There, AE-SVM generalizes only while one feature carries magnitude.

The study has limits. The simulator is not packet-level traffic. Both public
datasets have known labelling artifacts. We subsample them to 20,000 training
flows, which favours kernel methods. Kernel settings were not tuned per dataset.
Certified radii cover $\ell_2$ noise only, not adaptive evasion.

\section{Conclusion}

The amplitude-encoding kernel gives no in-distribution gain over an RBF kernel on
flow data. It needs more data at small $n$. On synthetic flows it fails on unseen
attacks because it discards the input norm. A one-coordinate norm anchor repairs
this. On UNSW-NB15 and CIC-IDS2017 the failure does not occur. Quantum and
quantum-style detectors should be tested on held-out attack families from real
traffic, with their invariances checked first.
